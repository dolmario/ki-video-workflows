# Understanding resistors: calculate, select and measure safely

Original English narration. Calculated values are theoretical predictions, not recorded measurements. Practical activities are proposed teaching exercises that have not been performed for this film. Ten chapters; actual runtime must be verified after narration. Editorial date: 2026-10-06.

## 1 | The assignment

### 01 | From a component to a working circuit
A small resistor has only two terminals. Nevertheless, it can determine whether current is limited appropriately, whether a voltage is correct, and whether a component becomes too hot. Today we connect three skills: identifying a value, calculating a circuit, and evaluating a measurement. By the end, you should be able to explain why a loaded voltage divider produces a different output voltage, and why reading the resistance correctly is not enough to select a suitable component. We begin with five volts of direct current supply. The numbers shown in the worked examples are theoretical predictions. They are not measurements recorded by us. Eight proposed experiments can later be completed under supervision. The blank worksheets are reserved for your actual observations and measured results.

### 02 | Plan before switching on
The proposed circuits use the isolated low-voltage output of a suitable laboratory power supply. The enclosure stays closed; this lesson involves no work on the mains side. Switch the output off before changing any connection. Check component values, wiring, meter sockets, measurement range, and current limit. Work in pairs: one person assembles the circuit while the other compares it with the diagram and records the steps. Then exchange roles. A current limit is helpful, but it does not replace correct wiring or a calculation of resistor power. Do not begin with unknown or damaged equipment. Have the actual components and instruments checked by your instructor. This film explains a learning activity; it does not present a practical setup that has already passed inspection.

### 03 | Keep prediction and observation separate
Imagine a one-kilohm resistor connected across five volts. What current do you expect? Write the calculation and its unit before taking a real measurement. The worksheet has separate spaces for those two steps. If the later measurement differs, do not edit it to match the prediction. Consider tolerance, the instrument, temperature, connections, and the actual voltage across the component. Good documentation shows both what was predicted and what was genuinely observed. A plausible number does not prove that an experiment took place. Likewise, a table filled entirely from calculations is not a measurement series. We maintain this distinction throughout the lesson. It makes troubleshooting easier because the assumptions behind each expectation can be checked explicitly, rather than silently treated as established facts.

### 04 | Use a complete working sequence
Our working sequence is to gather information, plan, choose, carry out, check, and evaluate. Gathering information includes reading markings, the datasheet, and the instrument manual. Planning means defining the circuit and measurement points. Choosing means explaining the resistance, construction, and power rating. Carrying out requires a controlled assembly; checking compares predicted and actual results. Evaluation means explaining a difference and suggesting an improvement. Equations alone do not cover all those steps. Take a moment to identify what information can be read directly from a resistor and what must be looked up. A marking may identify resistance. It does not automatically identify a dependable power rating, the maximum permitted voltage, or the relevant mounting conditions. Those additional properties matter before a component is used in a powered circuit.

## 2 | Voltage, current and resistance

### 05 | Three quantities and their units
We label voltage with U and measure it in volts. Current is I, measured in amperes. Resistance is R, measured in ohms. One kilohm equals one thousand ohms; one milliampere equals one thousandth of an ampere. Conversion is part of the calculation, not an optional detail. Five volts divided by one thousand ohms gives zero point zero zero five amperes, or five milliamperes. Entering one instead of one thousand produces an answer that is a thousand times too large. Include the units when substituting values. Later, kilohms and milliamperes can be convenient if you understand their relationship. For the initial checking method, we deliberately use volts, ohms, and amperes. Keeping the unit alongside every number also makes it much easier to locate a factor-of-a-thousand mistake in a longer calculation.

### 06 | What Ohm’s law actually describes
For an ohmic resistor at approximately constant temperature, voltage equals resistance multiplied by current. Rearranging gives current equals voltage divided by resistance. Our example still uses five volts and one kilohm. Doubling the voltage to ten volts doubles the calculated current to ten milliamperes. Instead, doubling the resistance while keeping five volts halves the current to two point five milliamperes. The statement has conditions. It does not mean that every electrical component has a constant ratio of voltage to current. A light-emitting diode behaves differently, and a temperature-dependent resistor can change its value considerably. Identify the model before applying the equation. A correct mathematical rearrangement cannot compensate for using a model that does not describe the component or operating conditions in front of you.

### 07 | Read the characteristic curve correctly
Our horizontal axis represents voltage and the vertical axis represents current. With one kilohm, the nominal predictions at one, two, three, four, and five volts are one, two, three, four, and five milliamperes. A straight line through the origin represents the idealized ohmic model here. Its slope is current divided by voltage, which is conductance, not resistance. Five milliamperes divided by five volts gives one millisiemens. Resistance is the reciprocal of that conductance. Reversing the axes changes the meaning of the slope. Always label axes, units, and actual measurement points explicitly. The straight line shown in this film is calculated; it is not a curve fitted to measurements collected by us. A real set of points must be recorded separately and evaluated under its actual measurement conditions.

### 08 | Prepare experiment two
For the proposed characteristic-curve experiment, use a known one-kilohm resistor rated for at least a quarter watt. Set the current limit to ten milliamperes and keep the voltage across the resistor at or below five volts. An ammeter goes in series; a voltmeter goes in parallel with the resistor. If only one suitable meter is available, plan separate measurements and switch the supply output off when changing connections. The voltage actually measured at the resistor matters, rather than just the power-supply display. Calculate resistance from each pair of values. Does it remain approximately constant? Otherwise, first check connections, measurement range, current limiting, and heating. The proposed practical activity takes twenty-five minutes outside this film. Resume afterwards to compare your genuinely recorded table with the theoretical predictions.

## 3 | Value, tolerance and colour bands

### 09 | Nominal value is not measured value
One kilohm identifies a nominal value of one thousand ohms. With a five-percent tolerance, the permitted initial deviation is fifty ohms in either direction. The resulting range is nine hundred and fifty to one thousand and fifty ohms. Component tolerance is not an instrument error, nor does it guarantee that any meter displays the true value exactly. Real measurement also involves instrument accuracy, contacts, and temperature. A result close to a tolerance limit therefore needs careful evaluation. With a one-percent tolerance, the range would be nine hundred and ninety to one thousand and ten ohms. First calculate the absolute deviation, then calculate both boundaries. This prevents a percentage from being mistaken for a fixed number of ohms. Record the tolerance and the measurement conditions as separate pieces of information.

### 10 | Decode four bands step by step
The digit colours start with black for zero, brown for one, and red for two. Orange means three, yellow four, green five, blue six, violet seven, grey eight, and white nine. With four bands, the first two colours supply two digits. The third band is the multiplier and the fourth specifies tolerance. Yellow, violet, red, gold therefore means forty-seven multiplied by one hundred ohms, with five-percent tolerance. That gives four thousand seven hundred ohms, or four point seven kilohms. In this position, gold means five percent; it is not an additional digit. A colour can have different functions depending on its position. Read the complete sequence and use the appropriate marking table, rather than assigning a single meaning to each colour regardless of where it appears.

### 11 | Low values and five-band markings
Gold as a multiplier means multiply by zero point one. Brown, black, gold, gold gives ten multiplied by zero point one ohms: one ohm, with five-percent tolerance. In that example gold serves once as multiplier and once as tolerance. A five-band marking begins with three significant digits instead. Brown, black, black, red, brown means one hundred multiplied by one hundred ohms: ten kilohms, with one-percent tolerance. Spacing or the position of a tolerance band can help establish reading direction, but it is not sufficient when a colour is damaged or unclear. Check possible reading directions, product information, and a resistance measurement with the component unpowered. Do not install an unidentified resistor in a powered circuit on the basis of a guessed colour sequence.

### 12 | The sixth band and your first table
For suitable six-band products, the final band can identify the temperature coefficient. In the Vishay table used here, a final red band can mean fifty parts per million per kelvin. This marking is product-dependent, not a universal extension of every colour chart. Brown, black, black, red, brown, red represents ten kilohms, one-percent tolerance, and the stated temperature marking in the appropriate example. Your first experiment needs three known resistors, a colour chart, magnification, and a suitable meter. Record the colour sequence, nominal value, and tolerance interval, then measure each component individually with no external power applied. Allow twenty-five minutes for the activity. Leave genuine measurement fields empty until measurements exist. Unclear markings should be documented and investigated rather than quietly converted into confident answers.

## 4 | Construction, SMD and preferred values

### 13 | Equal resistance does not mean equal properties
Carbon-film, metal-film, metal-oxide, and wirewound resistors can share a nominal resistance while having different properties. A resistive film on a support differs from a winding of resistance wire. A suitable carbon-film product may be sufficient for an uncomplicated learning circuit. Tighter stability requirements lead you to consider appropriate metal-film or thin-film products. Metal-oxide and wirewound families include power variants. This is neither a universal quality ranking nor a fixed wattage for each construction. A wire winding can also be inductive, which matters for some alternating-current applications. The complete product number and its datasheet are decisive. Body colour alone does not reliably establish material, power handling, or precision. Identify the requirements of the actual application before choosing the actual product that must meet them.

### 14 | Read small surface-mount components
SMD resistors mount directly onto a circuit board. Thick film and thin film identify different manufacturing technologies, rather than simply large and small parts. Under the common three-digit system, the marking one zero three means ten multiplied by ten to the power three ohms: ten kilohms. Under the four-digit system, one zero zero two means one hundred multiplied by ten to the power two ohms, also ten kilohms. Four R seven uses R as the decimal marker and means four point seven ohms. Not every small resistor has a readable marking at all. The code proves neither its wattage nor its guaranteed tolerance. Circuit-board conditions also influence heat removal. For an unmarked component, obtain the bill of materials, circuit information, or reliable product identification before treating a measured resistance as a complete specification.

### 15 | A table index is not an ordinary number
EIA ninety-six uses two digits as an index into a table and a letter as a multiplier. That differs from the ordinary numeric multiplier we just examined. In the Bourns table used for this lesson, zero one A means one hundred ohms, while one zero C means twelve point four kilohms. The index ten is therefore not simply interpreted as ten ohms. Use the table appropriate to the manufacturer and product, and record the source. A marking system can be used across different tolerance families, so the code does not replace a datasheet. When identification remains uncertain, the appropriate decision is to withhold the component from use until it is clarified. For very small SMD packages, mounting space and thermal conditions may determine suitability more strongly than nominal resistance alone.

### 16 | Select preferred values deliberately
E-series specify preferred resistance values within a decade. E six includes the base values ten, fifteen, twenty-two, thirty-three, forty-seven, and sixty-eight. Multiplication by powers of ten produces additional values. Finer series provide more intermediate choices, making it easier to implement a calculated requirement using an available component. Nevertheless, E-series membership and guaranteed tolerance are separate specifications. Consult an actual manufacturer table rather than reconstructing every listed value from a roughly rounded formula. If your calculation falls between available nominal values, examine the candidates together with tolerance and power limits. A closer nominal value is not, by itself, a complete selection argument. Explain which property is decisive in your circuit, and check the consequences of using the nearest available value before accepting it.

## 5 | Measure safely

### 17 | Check sockets before every measurement
For voltage or resistance measurements, leads belong in COM and the appropriate voltage-resistance socket. For current, use the correctly fused current socket and range identified in the instrument manual. An ammeter is inserted in series into the relevant current path. It must never simply be connected across the source as though it were a voltmeter; that can cause a short circuit. After measuring current, return the lead to the voltage-resistance socket. Check the sockets before each measurement, even if the previous measurement worked correctly. An instrument fuse does not replace proper connection. Keep the supply output off while changing wiring, then compare the setup with the circuit diagram. Switch on only after those checks and only for the intended measurement step. Correct instrument selection and an intact lead set are also part of the preparation.

### 18 | Measure resistance with external power removed
In resistance mode, the meter applies its own small test quantity. An external supply can corrupt the reading and endanger the instrument. Switch the circuit off, disconnect its external supply, and safely deal with stored energy using the appropriate procedure before measuring resistance. Consider parallel paths: a connected second branch can contribute to the reading. Two one-kilohm resistors in parallel ideally give five hundred ohms. That does not mean that either resistor has suddenly been manufactured incorrectly. Disconnecting one branch allows the individual value to be examined. At low resistance, leads and contacts affect the result; at high resistance, fingers and moisture can create additional paths. Document the real setup so that somebody else can understand exactly what the instrument was connected to and what the displayed value represents.

### 19 | A voltmeter is also a load
A voltmeter has a finite input resistance. This worked example explicitly assumes ten megohms; the corresponding specification must be checked in the manual of the real instrument. Our divider has two one-megohm resistors across five volts. Without a meter, the ideal calculation predicts two point five volts. The meter’s ten megohms, however, appear in parallel with the lower resistor. Their combined resistance is approximately zero point nine zero nine megohms. Recalculating the divider gives about two point three eight one volts. The instrument changes the circuit whose voltage it is intended to report. A different input resistance requires a new calculation. This number is a model result with an explicit assumption, not a reading collected from a particular instrument. Keeping that assumption visible prevents a calculated loading effect from being mistaken for universal meter behaviour.

### 20 | Check the measurement plan
Experiment seven has two separate parts. First investigate the high-resistance voltage divider using an instrument with a known input resistance. Then inspect two one-kilohm resistors in parallel, followed by one disconnected branch, using resistance mode with no external supply. Record a prediction, a real measurement, and an explanation for each step. The proposed thirty minutes include connection checks and evaluation. Measurement categories such as CAT two, three, and four describe environments and transient withstand requirements; they are not simple accuracy grades. A high category does not authorize learners to perform arbitrary mains experiments. We remain at isolated low-voltage outputs. If a value is surprising, check the assumptions and connections first. An extra decimal place on a display is not evidence that the measurement is accurate to that additional digit.

## 6 | Series circuits

### 21 | One path carries the same current
A simple series circuit has one closed current path. Current through the first and second resistors is consequently the same. The first resistor does not consume part of that current. Electrical energy is converted into heat while we consider the current in their shared path. Our example uses one kilohm and two point two kilohms across five volts. Series resistances add to three point two kilohms. Five volts divided by three thousand two hundred ohms gives one point five six two five milliamperes. That current applies to both resistors. A different current reading would suggest that you are not examining the same simple series arrangement, or that the measurement conditions and connections require investigation. Follow the actual continuous path through the diagram instead of assuming that visually adjacent components must be in series.

### 22 | Divide voltage and check the total
Voltage across the first resistor equals the shared current multiplied by its resistance. That gives one point five six two five volts across the one-kilohm resistor. The second resistor receives three point four three seven five volts. Adding both voltages returns the five volts of the source. At the same current, the larger resistance receives the larger voltage. Mark the current path and the reference points for both voltage measurements before moving leads. During a practical check, also compare their sum with the voltage actually measured at the source. Rounding and instrument uncertainty can explain small differences. An unexpected sign or a substantial discrepancy requires investigation of connections, rather than changing recorded numbers until the sum looks neat. Your diagram and written reference points make that investigation reproducible.

### 23 | Calculate power for each component
Even with a small total, check the dissipation of each resistor separately. Power is voltage multiplied by current. The first resistor dissipates approximately two point four four milliwatts; the second approximately five point three seven milliwatts. Their sum agrees with the source power of five volts multiplied by one point five six two five milliamperes. These predictions use nominal resistor values. Real components introduce tolerances. In this teaching example, components rated for at least a quarter watt have considerably more nominal capability than the predicted dissipation, provided their datasheet conditions are met. That margin is part of our proposed design. It does not replace checks for another product, a higher ambient temperature, or a changed supply voltage. Recalculate when conditions change rather than assuming that a previous safe selection covers every later arrangement.

### 24 | Investigate a series circuit yourselves
Experiment three is planned for twenty minutes. Begin by drawing the supply and both resistors in series, then calculate their common current and individual voltages. Assemble with the output switched off and set a ten-milliampere current limit. Each change of measurement type requires switching off again and checking the meter sockets. Your evaluation answers three questions: Is current consistent throughout the shared path? Do the individual voltages agree with the prediction within the measurement conditions? Does their sum agree with the source? If an open connection produces no current, that first describes an interrupted circuit. A single displayed voltage does not then prove that both resistors are correctly connected. Use the complete diagram systematically to locate the interruption and document the correction, instead of drawing conclusions from one isolated reading.

## 7 | Loaded voltage dividers

### 25 | Start with the unloaded divider
Two equal one-kilohm resistors across five volts produce two point five volts at the midpoint relative to the negative terminal in the ideal unloaded case. Total resistance is two kilohms and current is two point five milliamperes. Equal resistors receive equal voltages. The familiar divider equation is supply voltage multiplied by the lower resistance, divided by the sum of both resistances. The reference point is crucial. Output voltage does not mean a voltage between any arbitrary pair of terminals. Explicitly identify the midpoint and the negative terminal. Our first calculation assumes no additional load and an ideal measuring instrument. A consumer or a finite meter resistance changes those assumptions. We will now examine that change step by step, so that the equation remains attached to the circuit it actually describes.

### 26 | The load creates a parallel branch
Connect an additional one-kilohm resistor from the output to the negative terminal. This load is parallel with the lower divider resistor. Two equal one-kilohm resistors in parallel give five hundred ohms. The upper resistor remains one kilohm, so the source now sees one thousand five hundred ohms overall. Current increases to approximately three point three three three milliamperes. Output voltage falls to approximately one point six six seven volts. The load was not added in series at the end of the circuit; it changes the lower branch explicitly. Continuing to expect two point five volts means calculating the previous arrangement. A passive divider therefore does not provide an unchanged stable supply for varying loads. Analyse the divider together with the connected consumer, rather than treating the consumer as an afterthought that leaves the divider unaffected.

### 27 | Check branch currents and power
The upper resistor now receives approximately three point three three three volts and carries approximately three point three three three milliamperes. Its dissipation is about eleven point one one milliwatts. The lower resistor and equal load each receive approximately one point six six seven volts. Their individual currents are each approximately one point six six seven milliamperes, and each dissipates about two point seven seven eight milliwatts. The two branch currents add back to the current through the upper resistor. This check connects the divider calculation with the node-current rule. Also verify the power balance. A calculation claiming more dissipated power than the source provides signals a problem with values, units, or the circuit model. Retain adequate intermediate precision and round the final displayed results rather than accumulating large rounding errors during every step.

### 28 | Predict a change of load
In experiment four, measure first without the load and then with the additional one-kilohm load. Switch the supply output off before changing the circuit. Keep the current limit at ten milliamperes and use components rated for at least a quarter watt. Allow thirty minutes for calculation, controlled assembly, and evaluation. For transfer, ask what happens as load resistance becomes very large. It loads the lower branch less, and output approaches the unloaded value. A smaller load resistance reduces the lower equivalent resistance and decreases output further. That is a qualitative prediction, not permission to connect arbitrary loads. Calculate current and dissipation before powering any changed circuit. Keep the measurement spaces empty until you have performed the actual activity. A predicted trend is useful precisely because your real observations can later support it or reveal an overlooked condition.

## 8 | Parallel circuits and conductance

### 29 | Equal voltage, different branch currents
In a parallel circuit, both ends of each branch connect to the same two nodes. Every branch therefore receives the same voltage, while its current may differ. Our first branch is four hundred and seventy ohms, the second one kilohm, and the third two point two kilohms. All are connected across five volts. Calculate each current separately as voltage divided by resistance: approximately ten point six three eight milliamperes, five milliamperes, and two point two seven three milliamperes. At the same voltage, the smallest resistance carries the largest current. Identify the two common nodes before adding anything. Resistors are not parallel merely because they are drawn next to each other. The electrical connections, rather than the decorative placement of their symbols, determine which calculation describes the arrangement correctly.

### 30 | Why resistance values do not simply add
For parallel branches, add conductances. Conductance equals one divided by resistance and is measured in siemens. Our three branches contribute approximately two point one two eight, one, and zero point four five five millisiemens. Their sum is approximately three point five eight two millisiemens. Taking the reciprocal gives an equivalent resistance of approximately two hundred and seventy-nine point one six ohms. This is below the smallest individual branch resistance, which provides a strong plausibility check. Adding four hundred and seventy, one thousand, and two thousand two hundred ohms directly would calculate a series circuit. For two equal parallel resistors, remembering that resistance halves is convenient. With three unequal branches, the general conductance method is more reliable than trying to extend a shortcut beyond the situation it was designed for.

### 31 | Check total current and dissipation
The three branch currents sum to approximately seventeen point nine one one milliamperes. As a check, divide five volts by the equivalent resistance calculated earlier; the same total current should result. Nominal branch dissipations are approximately fifty-three point one nine, twenty-five, and eleven point three six milliwatts. If all resistances are five percent smaller, fixed-voltage power increases. The most demanding branch in this example reaches approximately fifty-five point nine nine milliwatts. Under those assumed tolerances, combined current remains below twenty milliamperes. This supports the proposed teaching setup’s current limit and component selection. Different supply voltages or resistor values require a new calculation. A table that was appropriate once does not remain appropriate after every change. Check the actual values, including relevant tolerances, before reusing a previously accepted power estimate.

### 32 | Measure a branch without shorting the supply
Experiment five is planned for twenty-five minutes. Use the three stated resistances, each rated for at least a quarter watt, with a five-volt supply and a twenty-milliampere current limit. To measure a branch current, switch the output off, open that particular branch, and insert the appropriate ammeter in series. Do not place the ammeter across the source. Recheck its socket, range, and wiring before switching on. Compare the sum of branch currents with total current and the calculated prediction. Also check whether current limiting has reduced the actual supply voltage below five volts. An unexpectedly small current could otherwise be incorrectly blamed on a resistor. Evaluation records differences and their measurement conditions, rather than writing predicted values beside a second copy of the same predicted values and calling that a successful experiment.

## 9 | Power, heat and temperature

### 33 | Ohms and watts answer different questions
Resistance describes the relationship between voltage and current. A wattage rating specifies a loading limit under defined conditions. A kilohm is not a kilowatt. Five volts across one kilohm gives five milliamperes and twenty-five milliwatts. Fifteen volts across five hundred ohms instead gives thirty milliamperes and zero point four five watts. A quarter-watt resistor would be overloaded in that second example. We only calculate that case; we do not build it using the unsuitable part. Another form of the power equation is voltage squared divided by resistance. At constant voltage, a smaller resistance therefore dissipates more power. At constant current, power instead equals current squared multiplied by resistance. Always state which quantity is held constant. Otherwise two apparently contradictory statements can both be correct under different conditions.

### 34 | Dissipated power is not a calculated temperature
The supervised heating activity calls for a suitable one-hundred-ohm power resistor rated for at least two watts. Three volts gives a nominal thirty milliamperes and zero point zero nine watts. Six volts gives sixty milliamperes and zero point three six watts: doubling voltage quadruples dissipation. That is an electrical calculation, not a temperature prediction. Surface temperature depends on construction, surroundings, attachment, time, and heat removal. Use a heat-resistant base and suitable holder; do not heat the part in a plastic breadboard or touch it. Set a seventy-milliampere current limit. The instructor must choose and check the actual product. Without that check, do not perform the activity. A suitable wattage figure alone cannot establish that the mounting arrangement is suitable or that the surface will stay at a particular temperature.

### 35 | Understand datasheet derating
Rated power is not independent of temperature. A datasheet may specify full power up to a temperature and then show a declining curve. This reduction is called derating. In the Bourns example examined for the research, permitted dissipation falls linearly from seventy to one hundred and fifty-five degrees Celsius. At their midpoint, one hundred and twelve point five degrees, half the rating remains. For the specified small product, that would mean zero point zero five watts rather than zero point one watts. This is a datasheet calculation, not a temperature we intend to produce in class. A separate maximum voltage can also apply. All relevant limits must be satisfied together. Do not transfer one manufacturer’s temperature curve to another resistor or to different mounting conditions merely because the nominal resistance is equal.

### 36 | Keep the heating activity bounded
Experiment six allows thirty minutes. After setup inspection, observe each of the three-volt and six-volt conditions for no more than sixty seconds. Record actual voltage, current, and temperature every fifteen seconds. Use a suitable insulated or non-contact temperature sensor according to its instructions. Immediately switch off for smoke, smell, unstable mounting, or a surface temperature above fifty degrees, and allow cooling. Fifty degrees is our conservative teaching stop threshold, not a guarantee that touching the component is safe. Nobody checks temperature using a finger. Worksheet entries remain empty until measured. Afterwards compare electrical power at the two voltage levels and describe the temperature variation that was genuinely observed. The result may also reveal that the chosen measuring arrangement needs improvement. An imperfect result honestly recorded is more useful than an invented smooth curve.

### 37 | Temperature coefficient as an approximation
A fixed resistor still changes resistance with temperature. Fifty parts per million per kelvin over a forty-kelvin temperature change gives a calculated zero point two percent change. At one kilohm that is two ohms; at ten kilohms it is twenty ohms. This temperature-coefficient calculation is an approximation within the specified temperature range. It is additional to initial tolerance, not another way of writing the same tolerance specification. A narrow nominal range provides little benefit if temperature and measurement conditions are uncontrolled. Include temperature conditions when comparing readings. Kelvin is used for the difference. A difference of forty degrees Celsius equals a difference of forty kelvin even though the absolute scales have different zero points. That distinction becomes particularly important when a later temperature-dependent equation requires an absolute temperature rather than just a temperature difference.

### 38 | An NTC becomes less resistive as it warms
An NTC is a temperature-dependent resistor with a negative temperature coefficient. Our proposed activity uses a specific ten-kilohm product that must be approved for the setup. The original approximation assumes ten kilohms at twenty-five degrees Celsius and a B value of three thousand nine hundred and seventy-seven kelvin. It predicts approximately six point four nine kilohms at thirty-five degrees, and four point three two kilohms at forty-five degrees. Temperature must be entered in kelvin in that equation. The manufacturer’s curve and tolerances take precedence over this approximation. We do not claim a measured curve. During resistance measurement the sensor has no external power supply. The meter’s test current must be small enough that self-heating does not significantly alter the temperature and resistance that the measurement is intended to describe.

### 39 | Experiment eight without an invented curve
Plan twenty-five minutes for experiment eight. Use a suitable known NTC, meter, thermometer, and a closed warm-water container. Warm the sensor indirectly while keeping electrical contacts dry. Do not immerse a coating that has not been approved for that use. There is no open flame or hot-air experiment. First observe when temperature and resistance readings have stabilized adequately, then enter the real values and compare them with the approximation. Differences can arise from the actual product, tolerances, temperature distribution, or self-heating. The datasheet specifies the appropriate conditions. Do not treat an NTC as a constant fixed resistor. Equally, a positive temperature coefficient does not automatically mean that every such component is linear over every temperature range. Match the model, the product information, and the actual activity rather than extending one convenient formula beyond its valid conditions.

## 10 | Troubleshooting and transfer

### 40 | Four checks before explaining a difference
When measurement disagrees with calculation, work systematically. First, were units converted correctly? Second, is the actual circuit the same circuit that was calculated? Third, is the expected voltage really across the component, or is the supply limiting current? Fourth, have sockets, ranges, and meter loading been considered? Only then discuss component tolerance, temperature, and other influences. Do not erase an initial result simply because a later check found a mistake. Record the first setup, the correction, and the new result clearly. This creates a useful troubleshooting record. A beautiful table of perfectly matching numbers is less helpful if nobody can identify the conditions under which they were obtained. Explanations belong to the result. They help another person reproduce the check and distinguish a wiring error from a genuine limitation of the original model.

### 41 | Complete your own final check
Test yourselves without looking at the earlier calculations. Decode yellow, violet, red, gold and state the value and both tolerance boundaries. Calculate current and power for five volts across one kilohm. Determine the equivalent resistance of two one-kilohm resistors in parallel. Calculate the equal-resistor voltage divider with an additional one-kilohm load. Explain why leaving a lead in the current socket can be dangerous during voltage measurement, and why resistance mode cannot be used with an energized source. Finally calculate dissipation for fifteen volts across five hundred ohms. The original worksheets separate questions from solutions. Our classroom assessment is not an official examination standard. A circuit that has actually been assembled also needs practical inspection; a correct written solution is not evidence that the wiring and instrument connections are safe.

### 42 | Transfer the reasoning to an LED
Finally, apply the principle to a light-emitting diode. A series resistor is calculated from the difference between supply voltage and assumed LED forward voltage, divided by the desired current. Five volts of supply, an assumed two volts across the LED, and ten milliamperes give a nominal three hundred ohms. This is intentionally a calculation based on assumptions, not a complete component approval. The actual LED type, current limits, supply tolerance, resistor tolerance, and resistor dissipation need additional checks. A typical forward voltage is not a guaranteed minimum. The maximum current listed in a datasheet is not automatically the correct operating current. This lesson stops at that transfer example; it does not create a separate LED experiment film. You now have a connected method for checking resistance, circuit arrangement, power, and measurement conditions, and for documenting why a component and setup are appropriate.
